Shortstops Galore: Why MLB Teams Love SS

Will Cahoon · @WillColqhn · June 5, 2026

Shortstops make up a quarter of our PROSPX top 100 batters, more than any other position. It comes down to rarity, athleticism, and how easily a shortstop can slide to almost anywhere else on the diamond.

If we break down our PROSPX top 100 batters by position, there’s a clear favorite. Shortstop! This makes some intuitive sense due to position value. Shortstop is a premium position, and so it makes sense that teams would select for it. However, it’s not the premium position. That would be a catcher. So, why do so many shortstops appear on our list compared to catchers? The answer is twofold: rarity and malleability. First, rarity. Catchers are rare. They’re overrepresented, sure, but they only make up 7 of our top 100 list compared to a whopping 25 shortstops.

The reality is that catching is really hard and requires a lot of skill to be good at. That’s why once you’re an established catcher, you can stick in the league well after your time should be up. Crazy but true: Sandy León had a 66 wRC+ in 2017 with the Red Sox. Every single year since León has been worse than that mark offensively, and yet he’s still actively getting playing time 8 years later.

Soft skills come into play here more than most other positions, requiring a great defensive catcher not only to work on physical skills, but also game calling and emotionally managing a pitcher. Those skills are harder to develop and don’t develop quickly for young players. This begs the next question: If catchers are in such short supply and are therefore valuable, why not just draft a catcher?

This leads us to our next problem with catchers: malleability. A key driver of what makes catching so valuable, but also simply not worth as much investment in, is the range of outcomes a catching prospect produces defensively. The question becomes, if X player can’t play catcher, where can they play next? That answer is almost always first base. If they can run, maybe outfield. Catchers often simply don’t have the physical ability to play middle infield or any of the other more valuable positions, and even if they do have the athleticism, they don’t have the reps.

Shortstop is the exact opposite. Since many teams will simply put their most athletic player at shortstop, many of the top position players drafted or signed due to their bat or physical attributes have started defensively at shortstop. Even Ceddanne Rafaela, arguably the best center fielder in major league baseball, was actually signed as a shortstop. Factors like reading the ball off the bat, range, and throwing from unique angles all have distinct overlaps with other defensive infield and outfield positions. Factors that make a good defensive catcher, on the other hand, such as framing, blocking, and pop time, are too unique to just catching.

So, drafting a shortstop means you could still be drafting your center fielder or second baseman of the future, even if the prospect has to move off their “natural” position.

These are similar players at first glance. Both are 60-grade athletes, power over hit tool, and fairly average defenders at their respective positions. So what separates the two defensively?

The first and easiest answer we can find is the run tool. De Vries is nothing special at 54, but Rodriguez really struggles here with a grade of just 33. So, while De Vries might be slightly better than average, Rodriguez is a full two standard deviations worse in roughly the bottom 5% of prospects. This is common for each position as well. An MLB catcher is, on average, 0.8 ft/s slower than the mean MLB sprint speed, whereas an SS is likely to be 0.8 ft/s faster.

This makes sense intuitively. Catchers simply don’t need to be fast. The area of coverage, even on a pop-up behind the plate, is by far the least out of any position. Being a good defensive catcher and being fast have almost nothing to do with each other. Patrick Bailey, Carlos Narvaez, and Austin Hedges are all incredible defenders, but they’re also incredibly slow. Shortstops sit somewhere in the middle. The position clearly rewards speed, with guys like Bobby Witt Jr and Nasim Nuñez having both elite defense and elite sprint speeds. However, since you simply don’t have as long to run for each play at short compared to an outfielder, speed is less of a requirement and more of a bonus compared to, say, center or right field. This is also why you’ll see faster catchers play left field over third base, since raw sprint speed translates better to the position.

So, our selected two players start to separate defensively based on the run tool. Even if the two players have never touched an outfield glove in their lives, we can already tell De Vries will likely have a much better outcome in the outfield compared to Rodriguez, simply because Rodriguez must become much more skilled than De Vries to get an equal defensive output.

Now, let’s get into the next major separator between the two: Frame. Both Rodriguez and De Vries can generate power, undeniably. Both players are 210 pounds and can sequence really well to turn that into both swing speed and arm strength. We see that reflected in each’s arm and power grade, respectively, about 55 and 60 for each, which is elite considering both are still teenagers. What’s not equal, though, is how they get there, and that’s where frame comes into play. Baseball is not simply about generating power; it’s about being able to generate power as fast as possible. Think of a player like a rocket ship. The engine can be massive and generate ridiculous amounts of power, but the engine itself doesn’t matter if the fuel makes the rocket too heavy to lift off the ground.

Rodriguez and De Vries may have similar-sized engines, but De Vries is able to use that engine a lot more effectively, requiring less “fuel” than Rodriguez. This means biomechanically, likely more of De Vries’ power generation is coming from his ability to use his frame and size compared to Rodriguez in general. De Vries sprint speed comes from being able to leverage that power much quicker than Rodriguez can, which implies that even with more muscle and a bigger engine than Rodriguez, De Vries will still be able to leverage that to actual force output over a small period of time, like a swing or throw.

With Rodriguez and his smaller frame, while he could certainly add mass and strength and increase his squat, that comes with more and more of a cost to his twitchiness and ability to use that force over a set period of time (rate over force). While the rate of force is a skill that a player can develop, fundamentally, adding more mass requires more force if you want to maintain the same acceleration. This doesn’t make it impossible for Rodriguez to develop more power in the future, and it certainly doesn’t guarantee that De Vries will add batspeed and muscle mass either, but it does start to give us a picture as to why you might favor the one profile over the other to add power without sacrificing twitchiness and speed long term.

That’s the crux of playing shortstop: quick reactions and movement, while still needing power to get the ball to first. Even further, it requires more athleticism. Body control, internal clock, and reaction speed are all essential, which is part of what makes a shortstop so incredibly hard in the modern game.

Shortstop is simply a better overall test of athleticism than catcher. This isn’t to say catchers aren’t athletic; they are, but the athleticism is much more specialized. This is again part of why we see selection bias at even the youth level for shortstops. Coaches are more likely to put the most athletic kid at short, so we have a long history of former shortstops who changed positions at the major league level.

Back to De Vries and Rodriguez, let’s project them as prospects with a little added mass and moving off their current positions. Let’s say for one reason or another, both can’t stick defensively and need to find a new home. For Rodriguez, our options are likely DH and first base. We can pretty much rule out the outfield as a home for him. The combination of being behind on routes and reads, and then well below average speed, would likely give him one of the worst jumps in major league baseball. 3B is possible (Josh Donaldson recently), but the transition is rare enough to be considered an outlier. So, let’s say Rodriguez transitions to first base nicely and ends up being a solid first base defender. On our PROSPX top 100 list, we have just two first basemen in the top 100: One is Josue Briceño, who still might end up catching, and the only true first basemen (despite also being moved off catcher) is Ralphy Velazquez. This highlights how hard it is to be considered a productive player at first. Briceño is on the IL after rocking a 147 OPS+ in high A last year, and here’s a look at how ridiculous Velazquez has looked so far this year.

This is a profile with contact, great power, and a good approach. He’s projected to be a quality major league hitter. First basemen need to do more than hit at the major league level: they need to have a complete offensive profile to be considered a good prospect, because if they can’t hit, they provide very little value.

Rodriguez looks to be the type of hitter who could make such a change (his O-tool is actually better than Velazquez), but first base still puts a lot of pressure on the bat to fully develop and limits his value long term. So, if Rodriguez does end up moving off catcher, it’s much more fully up to his ability to hit than to defend. First base defense also still isn’t easy. There’s no guarantee that it’s a smooth transition, which means once again the bat is crucial.
The outlook for De Vries is much brighter.

While the trends do seem as if he’s losing some athleticism and getting bigger, that pretty easily facilitates a move to third base. The PROSPX top 100 is much more kind towards third basemen, especially if they’re good defensively. Moving from shortstop to third is easier, and transitions away from needing as much athleticism and consistency, so it’s not a leap to say De Vries has the potential to be a good or even great third baseman. An example of this is Mets prospect Jacob Reimer.

Reimer has a defense grade of 45 and has quite a few holes in the profile. Yet, he’s still projected to be a productive player. Reimer isn’t ranked particularly high (#88 on PROSPX), but he still demonstrates the room for error generally not given to first basemen. So, even if De Vries moves off of short, he’s still going to be an elite prospect capable of providing defensive value. The range of outcomes for De Vries on defense is simply much better than for Rodriguez. He ends up being a safer prospect as a hitter because there are so many ways for him to make up for any value lost as a hitter. Rodriguez doesn’t get that luxury. This helps explain as well why, despite getting the same o-tool grade at 56 and similar d-tools with De Vries at 52 and Rodríguez at 51, De Vries is ranking much higher. De Vries is ranked 2nd on our PROSPX list, and Rodriguez is ranked 13th. If we used pure positional value to evaluate the two, we might expect Rodriguez to be ahead of De Vries. The fact that he’s not helps demonstrate how the position difference affects their overall range outcomes beyond just wherever the player is currently getting their defensive reps.

There’s also the shortstop to outfield pipeline, which, while De Vries is likely not doing, is something shortstops routinely excel at. For example, former excellent defender at short Javier Baez put up a good 2025 defensively, moving to center field despite no major league reps there in his 8 previous years. A current shortstop who might fit the mold in the minors is Luis Peña.

Everyone loves the bat, but the defense seems potentially a weak point in the profile. However, Peña is an excellent runner, potentially plus plus. The Brewers might want to consider getting Peña outfield reps, especially in center, where he can maximize his ability to run. Peña reportedly has struggled with lateral movement, and so in the outfield, you can minimize the impact of that and ideally maximize his burst. The outfield is a lot more forgiving of wrong first steps than the infield, so that might be a much more natural fit than shortstop. Playing center as well is another extremely valuable position, so once again, this potentially only slightly increases the pressure on the bat.

So, at the end of the day, baseball is going to keep loving shortstops. While public defensive evaluation in the minor leagues remains extremely limited, understanding defensive archetypes and developmental pathways can still tell us a great deal about how organizations value prospects. Defense is a major driver of major league value, and the uncertainty surrounding a player’s future defensive home is often just as important as their current performance.

The comparison between De Vries and Rodriguez illustrates this reality. Both players possess considerable offensive upside, but the range of defensive outcomes available to each is dramatically different. De Vries retains multiple realistic paths to providing defensive value even if he outgrows shortstop, while Rodriguez’s profile becomes increasingly dependent on the bat if he cannot remain at catcher. That difference in flexibility matters because every defensive step down the spectrum raises the offensive bar a player must clear to become a productive major leaguer.

Every prospect develops differently, and no player should be forced into a predetermined path. However, by understanding how athleticism, body type, and defensive position interact, we can better understand why organizations consistently place such a premium on shortstops and why the position continues to produce some of baseball’s most highly ranked prospects.
